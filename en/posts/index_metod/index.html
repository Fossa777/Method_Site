Traditions Instead of Science. I


Method.


Ziehl–Neelsen without a full set of reagents: what can we still learn?

If you have a fully equipped laboratory, all the necessary reagents, and proper working conditions, the answer is simple: to test bacterial acid-fastness, perform the standard Ziehl–Neelsen stain.

This text is not about replacing the standard method where the standard method is available.

It is about another situation — one that is much more common in small facilities, aquariums, among fish health specialists without a dedicated bacteriology laboratory, and even among aquarists working with a microscope. The diagnostic problem exists, the specimen is in front of you, but the complete set of reagents is not.

Phenol is toxic, unpleasant to work with, and requires appropriate storage and handling conditions.
Strong mineral acids are also not the kind of substances you necessarily want to keep “just in case” next to a microscope. They have to be obtained, stored, and handled with appropriate safety measures.

If part of the classical method is unavailable, does that mean the specimen can no longer provide any useful information?

What follows can be treated as a small methodological experiment: remove one step, replace another, and see exactly what changes. Or it can be treated as a very practical necessity.

In either case, the rule is the same:

You need to understand what information each step of the method provides, what remains after that step is changed, and which conclusion can no longer be made.



What does the classical method actually do?

Ziehl–Neelsen staining is used to detect acid-fast bacteria, especially mycobacteria (World Health Organization, 1982).

In the classical procedure, the specimen is stained with carbol fuchsin with heating, then decolorized with acid or acid-alcohol and counterstained with a contrasting dye (World Health Organization, 1982; World Health Organization, 1999.

The mycobacterial cell envelope is rich in lipids and mycolic acids and stains poorly with ordinary aqueous dyes (Caulfield & Wengenack, 2016; . Carbol fuchsin, phenol, and heating make it possible to achieve intense staining of these structures Caulfield & Wengenack, 2016; World Health Organization, 1982).

Then comes a fundamentally different step: the specimen is decolorized. Ordinary tissues and non-acid-fast bacteria lose the fuchsin, while acid-fast structures retain the red stain. Only after this step does the diagnostic feature of acid-fastness appear.

The counterstain is then used for ease of observation: it colors the background and makes the red rods easy to distinguish.

In other words, the classical sequence performs several different functions rather than a single one.


Knowing the limits of a method means being able to control it

If the method is treated as a recipe, everything is simple:

fuchsin → heating → acid → counterstain.

But if we break it down by function, the picture becomes more interesting:

  • carbol fuchsin provides intense staining;
  • phenol and heating help the dye penetrate structures that stain poorly;
  • acid decolorization tests the ability of stained structures to retain fuchsin;
  • counterstaining makes the background convenient for microscopy.

Therefore, removing one step does not necessarily destroy all information at once.

It removes a specific type of information.

Know the limits of the method — and you can control it.
Do not know the limits — and all that remains is to follow the recipe.

If bacteria are not visible, we do not know whether they are absent from the tissue or simply not being revealed by the method being used.
If they become visible after staining, that is already an observation. Their shape, abundance, distribution, and relationship to pathological lesions can be assessed.

But which property of those bacteria we are entitled to claim depends on which steps were actually performed.



What if there is no phenol?

Phenol is part of classical carbol fuchsin not because “that is the tradition.” It helps create conditions in which the dye can intensely stain lipid-rich structures. Heating serves a similar purpose by facilitating intense staining (World Health Organization, 1999; World Health Organization, 1982).

Phenol-free modifications of acid-fast staining also exist, and their diagnostic suitability has been specifically investigated (Ramos et al., 2019).

If phenol is unavailable, the question should not be:

“How can we still pretend that we are doing Ziehl–Neelsen?”

but rather:

Can the structures of interest be stained sufficiently by another means?

In my preparations, intensive heating with fuchsin without phenol made clearly visible red rod-shaped bacteria appear. This does not prove that phenol and heating are completely interchangeable. Nor does it make the procedure a classical Ziehl–Neelsen stain. But something important has happened for the practical task:

bacteria that were previously difficult to see became visible.

We can assess their morphology, abundance, and distribution in the tissue. In other words, part of the diagnostic information has been preserved.


What if there is no acid?

Here the boundary is even clearer. Acid decolorization is the step that creates the feature for which the method is called acid-fast: after strong differentiation, some structures lose fuchsin while others retain it. Therefore, if acid decolorization was not performed, one cannot claim:

“Acid-fast bacteria were detected.”

Acid-fastness simply was not tested. But that does not mean that everything done before this step becomes meaningless. If numerous rod-shaped bacteria become clearly visible in tissue after intense staining, we already know that they are there. We can see their shape, the density of their aggregates, their distribution, and their relationship to pathologically altered areas.

We simply do not yet know one additional property — whether they would retain fuchsin after acid differentiation. And those are fundamentally different things.

After intense staining with fuchsin, the specimen can be counterstained with a contrasting dye. In my case, a Löffler counterstain was used. The rods that had stained intensely earlier retained their bright red color.
This contrast makes the bacteria easy to see. But by itself it is not a test of acid-fastness.



Does it have to be exactly that acid?

Even the classical method shows that the important thing is not the “magic” of a particular acid, but the function of the step.

Different versions of acid-fast staining use different reagents and concentrations for differentiation: for example, sulfuric acid at different concentrations or acid-alcohol containing HCl (World Health Organization, 1982; World Health Organization, 1999). WHO materials have also mentioned hydrochloric, sulfuric, and nitric acids (World Health Organization, n.d.).

Therefore, the diagnostic meaning of the step lies primarily in sufficient decolorization, after which the structures of interest retain fuchsin.

This leads to a perfectly reasonable experimental question:

Try decolorizing with a concentrated citric acid solution and see what happens to the structures that were stained beforehand.

If the background and ordinary structures lose the stain while some rods retain fuchsin, that is already an observation that can be investigated further.

Important: this is an experimental test of the function of the step, not a reference to a validated Ziehl–Neelsen staining protocol. In the standard guidelines reviewed, citric acid is not listed as a routine decolorizer.

Therefore, concentration is not a sacred number from a recipe either; it is one of the conditions of differentiation. With weak treatment, some fuchsin will remain in surrounding tissues — and for the purpose of simply visualizing bacteria, that is not necessarily a problem. As treatment becomes stronger, the background will decolorize more extensively. What matters is understanding under which conditions, and how reliably, the structures of interest retain the stain.

The task is not to find the single “correct” acid concentration in the abstract. The task is to understand what changes when the strength of decolorization changes.



What do we actually know in the end?

Imagine a specimen in which numerous thin red rods become clearly visible after heating with fuchsin.

We can say that stained rod-shaped bacteria are present in the tissue.

We can assess:

  • their shape and size;
  • their arrangement;
  • the abundance and density of aggregates;
  • the tissue context;
  • their association with granulomatous or other pathological changes;
  • the reproducibility of the finding in different areas of the material.

Morphology alone, of course, does not determine the bacterial genus, let alone the species.

But the lack of complete identification does not make the observation useless.

Seeing bacteria and identifying their species are different diagnostic tasks.

Likewise:

seeing stained bacteria and demonstrating their acid-fastness are also different tasks.

If a higher level of certainty is needed, a complete acid-fast stain, culture, PCR, or another confirmatory method can be performed.

But if that option is not currently available, that is no reason to pretend that the microscope showed nothing at all.


Where are the “traditions instead of science” here?

The problem is not the standard method.
The standard method is valuable precisely because its conditions are defined, tested, and reproducible.
The problem begins when a sequence of actions is no longer understood as a set of operations with specific functions and instead turns into a ritual:

“Without phenol, nothing can be seen.”
“It has to be this exact acid because that is what the protocol says.”
“If one step is missing, the entire result automatically becomes worthless.”

A scientific approach requires a different question:

What exactly changed in the result because we changed the method?

If acid decolorization was removed, we lost the right to claim acid-fastness.
But we did not automatically lose the ability to see bacteria.
If we changed the way the dye penetrates the specimen, we need to check whether the visualization is sufficient.
If we replaced the differentiating reagent, we need to check whether it performs the required function.

And every time, the boundary is not between a “correct” and an “incorrect” ritual, but between what was actually tested and what we merely assume.

A method can be changed. But when the method changes, the limits of permissible conclusions change with it.



How should the result be described?

If the classical procedure was not performed in full, we can no longer hide behind its name.

It is acceptable to say:
“A modified heated fuchsin stain without acid decolorization was used. Intensely stained rod-shaped bacteria were detected in the tissue material.”

It is not acceptable to say:
“Ziehl–Neelsen staining was performed; acid-fast bacteria were detected.”

These are different statements.


Conclusion

Knowing the limits of a method means being able to control it.

If a method has been modified, there is no need either to present it as the standard method or to automatically declare the information obtained useless.

A simpler thing is needed:

state exactly what was done, what was observed, and what genuinely follows from it.
