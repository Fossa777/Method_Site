Tradition Instead of Science I. The Method.


The Method.


Knowing the limitations of a method does more than protect us from incorrect conclusions — it tells us where and how to look for what we cannot yet see.

Fossa

Ziehl–Neelsen staining without the full set of reagents: what can we still learn?

If you have a fully equipped laboratory, all the necessary reagents, and suitable working conditions, the answer is straightforward: to test whether bacteria are acid-fast, perform the standard Ziehl–Neelsen stain.

This article is not about replacing the standard method when it is available.

It is about a different situation—one much more familiar to small fish farms, public aquariums, fish pathologists without a dedicated bacteriology laboratory, and even aquarists who own a microscope. There is a diagnostic question, a specimen in front of you, but not the complete set of reagents.

Phenol is toxic, unpleasant to handle, and requires appropriate storage and working conditions.
Strong mineral acids are not exactly things you want to keep next to a microscope “just in case,” either. They have to be obtained, stored, and handled with proper safety precautions.

If part of the classic method is unavailable, does that mean the specimen can no longer tell us anything useful?

You can treat what follows as a little methodological experiment: remove one step, replace another, and see what actually changes. Or you can treat it as a practical necessity.

Either way, the principle is the same:

We need to understand what happens at each step, what that step allows us to learn, and what we can no longer conclude if we remove or change it.



What does the classic method actually do—and where did it come from?

Ziehl–Neelsen staining is used to detect acid-fast bacteria, particularly mycobacteria (World Health Organization, 1982).

Interestingly, today’s familiar procedure did not appear all at once. What we now call the “Ziehl–Neelsen method” emerged over a remarkably short period through successive changes to existing staining techniques (Vilchèze & Kremer, 2017).

In March 1882, Robert Koch, presenting his work on the causative agent of tuberculosis, described staining specimens with a hot alkaline solution of methylene blue, followed by counterstaining with vesuvin (Bismarck brown). The mycobacteria appeared blue against a brown background (Vilchèze & Kremer, 2017).

In May of that same year, Paul Ehrlich modified the method. Instead of methylene blue, he used fuchsin or methyl violet in saturated aniline water, then decolorized the specimen with 30% nitric acid and applied a counterstain (Vilchèze & Kremer, 2017).

The next step came from Franz Ziehl. In August 1882, he replaced aniline oil with carbolic acid—phenol. The resulting carbol fuchsin was more stable than Ehrlich’s aniline-based solution (Vilchèze & Kremer, 2017).

In October 1882, Eduard Rindfleisch proposed heating the specimen directly during staining.

Then, in July 1883, Friedrich Neelsen published his modification of Ziehl’s method: 0.75% fuchsin in 5% carbolic acid, followed by decolorization with 25% sulfuric acid (Vilchèze & Kremer, 2017).

So phenol did not enter the procedure as an immutable part of some original recipe.
Ziehl introduced it in place of aniline, Rindfleisch added heating, and Neelsen changed the stain formulation and the decolorization conditions.

Over roughly sixteen months, Koch’s original procedure underwent several substantial modifications before eventually becoming established under the familiar name Ziehl–Neelsen staining (Vilchèze & Kremer, 2017).

And the method did not stop evolving there.

In 1887, Gabbett attempted to shorten the procedure by combining acid decolorization with counterstaining. In 1915, Joseph Kinyoun proposed a “cold” method without heating: the fuchsin concentration was increased to approximately 3.1%, phenol to 6.25%, and 3% hydrochloric acid was used as the decolorizer (Vilchèze & Kremer, 2017).

Even historically, then, this is how the method developed:

The task remained the same, but the way of accomplishing it changed: reagents were replaced, concentrations adjusted, and exposure conditions modified.


What do we call the classic procedure today?

In modern variants, the specimen is stained with heated carbol fuchsin, then decolorized with acid or acid-alcohol and counterstained (World Health Organization, 1982) (World Health Organization, 1999).

One commonly used decolorizer is 3% acid-alcohol:

3 mL of concentrated hydrochloric acid + 97 mL of 95% ethanol.

In this formulation, “3%” refers to the conventional volume ratio of the starting concentrated acid to alcohol, not the exact mass fraction of pure HCl (World Health Organization, 1998).

But this is not Neelsen’s original formula. His 1883 method used 25% sulfuric acid.

The same applies to the primary stain. Neelsen used 0.75% fuchsin in 5% phenol. The composition was subsequently modified many times: Vilchèze and Kremer describe 0.3% fuchsin and 5% phenol for one modern ZN formulation, whereas Kinyoun’s cold method uses substantially higher concentrations (Vilchèze & Kremer, 2017).

And this matters for understanding the method:

There is no single concentration forever fixed to the name “Ziehl–Neelsen.” There is a purpose served by each step, and there are conditions under which that step produces a reproducible result.


Knowing a method’s limits means being able to control it

If we treat the method as a recipe, it is simple:

fuchsin → heat → acid → counterstain.

But if we ask why each of these steps is performed, things become more interesting:

  • carbol fuchsin provides intense staining;
  • phenol and heat help the dye penetrate structures that are otherwise difficult to stain;
  • acid decolorization tests whether stained structures can retain fuchsin;
  • counterstaining makes the background easier to examine microscopically.

That is why omitting one step does not necessarily make the entire result worthless.

Some observations remain possible, even though certain conclusions can no longer be drawn.

Know the limits of a method, and you can work with it.
Ignore those limits, and all you can do is follow the recipe.

If bacteria are not visible, we do not know whether they are absent from the tissue or simply cannot be detected by the method we used.
If staining makes them visible, that is already an observation. We can assess their shape, abundance, distribution, and relationship to pathological lesions.

But what this observation actually demonstrates depends on how the image was obtained. We can see red rods; whether they would retain fuchsin after acid treatment is another question.



What if there is no phenol?

Phenol is part of classic carbol fuchsin for a reason, not merely “because that is how it has always been done.” Traditionally, it is considered to facilitate dye penetration through the lipid-rich mycobacterial cell envelope. Heating also promotes staining.

But does that really mean fuchsin cannot enter the bacterium without phenol?

This question had already been tested experimentally, long before my own experiments—and, particularly interestingly, with pathogens of fish as well.

In 2000, Vinnie Della Speranza and Rena Fail published a paper with the rather expressive title “Phenol Myth Unveiled! Were Ziehl-Neelsen and Kinyoun Mistaken?”

The authors initially tried replacing phenol with surfactants. Then they decided to see what would happen if the surfactants were removed as well, leaving only an aqueous solution of basic fuchsin.

The result was unexpected: a 0.5% aqueous fuchsin solution heated to 60 °C produced good staining of mycobacteria in 15 minutes. At room temperature, the same solution remained ineffective even after an hour of exposure. The paper includes photomicrographs of Mycobacterium avium and M. tuberculosis stained without phenol (Della Speranza & Fail, 2000).

So removing phenol does not, in itself, prevent staining. The conditions under which the dye is applied still matter.

In 2008, Carol McCollough tested this approach specifically with pathogens of fish and aquatic invertebrates.

She used 0.5% aqueous fuchsin at 60 °C for 15 minutes, followed by acid-alcohol decolorization. The preparations revealed M. marinum in the kidney of a goldfish and M. chesapeaki in the spleen of a striped bass.

The study was not limited to mycobacteria. Positive staining was also obtained for several microsporidians, haplosporidians, and myxosporeans. Not every organism stained equally well: for example, fixed spores of Kudoa clupeidae did not retain fuchsin after decolorization (McCollough, 2008).

In other words, phenol-free staining had already been used in fish pathology to detect mycobacteria in fish tissues, including the spleen.

In 2020, Gomes and colleagues carried out another comparative study, introducing a modification called GASMoC.

Unlike the earlier experiments, they used 1% aqueous fuchsin at just 37 °C for 15 minutes. Specimens were stained in parallel: some with classic carbol fuchsin, others without phenol. Both groups then underwent the same acid-alcohol decolorization step.

Of 12 specimens with previously confirmed tuberculosis, GASMoC detected acid-fast bacteria in all 12, while the conventional Ziehl–Neelsen method detected them in 11. All seven negative controls remained negative with both methods.

The researchers found no statistically significant difference between the results. Moreover, bacilli stained using GASMoC were often more intensely colored and easier to distinguish against the surrounding tissue.

The paper provides direct comparative photomicrographs of the phenol-containing and phenol-free methods. The authors also successfully tested the method on zebrafish tissue containing Mycobacterium marinum (Gomes et al., 2020).

Thus, phenol was not essential for detecting acid-fast bacteria in the specimens examined. Changing the composition of the primary stain did not eliminate the diagnostically relevant result.

There is an important detail here: all three studies retained the acid decolorization step. The authors removed phenol but continued to test whether fuchsin was retained after acid treatment.

So this is not merely about seeing red rods without phenol. It is about detecting acid-fast structures using a modified primary stain.

Of course, this does not mean that any protocol can be changed arbitrarily while always producing the same result. But it does show that the same diagnostic characteristic can be detected under different staining conditions.

If phenol helps a dye enter a bacterium, that does not mean entry is impossible without phenol. The question is not whether the reagent is inherently indispensable, but whether the required result can be achieved without it.

If phenol is unavailable, we need not frame the question as:

“How can we pretend that we are still performing Ziehl–Neelsen staining?”

Instead, we can ask:

Can we achieve sufficient staining of the structures we want to examine by another means?

In my own preparations, intense heating with fuchsin without phenol made red, rod-shaped bacteria clearly visible. This does not prove that phenol and heat are fully interchangeable, nor does it turn the procedure into classic Ziehl–Neelsen staining. But something important did happen for the practical task:

Bacteria that had previously been difficult to distinguish became visible.

We can assess their morphology, abundance, and location within the tissue. That is useful information in its own right.


What if there is no acid?

Here the boundary is even clearer. It is the acid decolorization step that establishes the characteristic for which the method is called “acid-fast”: after stringent differentiation, some structures lose their fuchsin, while others retain it. Therefore, if no acid decolorization was performed, we cannot claim:

“Acid-fast bacteria were detected.”

Acid-fastness simply was not tested. But that does not make everything done before this step meaningless. If intensive staining makes numerous rod-shaped bacteria clearly visible in a tissue specimen, we now know they are present. We can observe their shape, the density of their clusters, their distribution, and their relationship to pathologically altered areas.

We simply do not yet know one particular property—whether they would retain fuchsin after acid differentiation. And those are fundamentally different things.

After intensive staining with fuchsin, the preparation can be counterstained. In my case, I used Loeffler’s counterstaining method. The rods that had initially taken up the stain intensely remained bright red.
This contrast makes rod-shaped bacteria easy to see. But by itself, it is not a test of acid-fastness.

And this opens up another possibility that is easy to overlook if we regard acid as a mandatory part of any fuchsin stain. Without acid decolorization, we do not see only structures capable of withstanding acid. Other bacteria, spores, parasitic oocysts, and various tissue structures may also remain stained. Sometimes that is a disadvantage: the background becomes more intensely colored, making the specimen harder to interpret. But at other times, this broader picture is precisely what we need.

By omitting acid decolorization, we lose selectivity for acid-fastness, but we may reveal structures that the acid step would have made invisible.

This is not an invented approach. For example, modified acid-fast fuchsin stains are used to detect the oocysts of Cryptosporidium, Cyclospora, and Cystoisospora. These are parasites, not fungi. In standard variants, acid decolorization is generally not omitted, but its conditions are modified; oocyst staining may be uneven, and some oocysts may remain completely unstained (CDC, n.d.) (CDC, n.d.).

Another example is bacterial endospores. Dorner’s method uses heated carbol fuchsin, and one modification works without a separate acid decolorization step. This is no longer a stain intended to detect mycobacteria: it uses a familiar dye to reveal a different target (American Society for Microbiology, 2016).

So the question is not always how to make a stain “correct” for detecting one particular pathogen. Sometimes it is more useful to reveal a wider range of structures first, assess their shape and location, and then decide which findings need specific confirmation.



One organ — three staining variants

For this comparison, I used material from the same spleen of an angelfish, which had numerous pale lesions.

Here is a gross photograph of the spleen.

From this material, I prepared specimens using three staining procedures:

  1. Without phenol and without acid decolorization.

  2. Without phenol, but with decolorization using 5% sulfuric acid.

  3. With phenol, followed by acid decolorization.

The same microscope and camera were used in every case.

The result was interesting: rod-shaped bacteria of similar morphology were clearly visible in all three preparations. Moreover, the rods retained their staining after treatment with 5% sulfuric acid even when phenol had been omitted.

In separate earlier experiments, I also observed rods retaining their stain after 30 minutes of exposure to 10% sulfuric acid.

Of course, three preparations from one organ do not constitute a full comparative study of method sensitivity. But they allow us to see directly that omitting phenol does not necessarily prevent staining the bacteria of interest.

And here is the central question: if we can obtain the result we need after changing the reagent composition, what is truly essential to the method, and what is merely one way of achieving the goal?

This does not mean that phenol is useless or that all three variants are equivalent. It means that the result can be examined, compared, and evaluated, rather than merely checked against a recipe.



Does it have to be that particular acid?

Even the history of the classic method shows that the important thing is not the “magic” of one specific acid, but how it decolorizes the specimen and whether the structures of interest retain their staining.

Different acid-fast staining protocols use different reagents and concentrations for differentiation: for example, sulfuric acid at various concentrations or acid-alcohol containing HCl ( World Health Organization, 1982; World Health Organization, 1999). WHO materials also mention the use of hydrochloric, sulfuric, and nitric acids (World Health Organization, n.d.).

The purpose of acid treatment is to distinguish structures that lose their staining readily from those that retain fuchsin under specified conditions. But changing the acid, its concentration, or the duration of treatment does not automatically produce the same diagnostic test.

This leads to a perfectly reasonable experimental question:

What happens if we use a concentrated citric acid solution instead of the usual decolorizer? Which structures lose their staining, and which retain it?

If the background becomes decolorized while some rods remain red, that is an interesting experimental observation. But this observation alone does not establish acid-fastness according to the standard criterion.

Important: this is an experimental substitution of a reagent, not a validated Ziehl–Neelsen variant. The standard manuals considered here do not list citric acid as a routine decolorizer; comparability of the results must be established using control preparations.

So concentration is not some sacred number written in a recipe. The type of acid and the duration of exposure matter as well. With milder treatment, fuchsin may remain in the surrounding tissues; if the goal is simply to see the rods, this is not always a problem. With more aggressive decolorization, even the bacteria of interest may lose their staining. We need to understand what the rods actually withstand, rather than treating every trace of retained red staining as proof of the same property.

The goal is not to discover the one “correct” acid concentration in every situation. The goal is to understand what happens when the stringency of decolorization changes.



Even acid-fastness is not a constant property

We are accustomed to describing mycobacteria as “acid-fast bacteria,” as though this were an unchanging property of every cell at every moment of its existence.

But that is not entirely true.

Vilchèze and Kremer discuss the so-called “Koch paradox”: when Mycobacterium tuberculosis enters a persistent, nonreplicating state, some bacteria can become ZN-negative—no longer detectable by standard acid-fast staining (Vilchèze & Kremer, 2017). This state is often called dormant, or “sleeping,” although the bacterium is not literally asleep.

This has been linked to changes in lipid metabolism and cell-envelope structure. The exact mechanism behind the loss of acid-fast staining, however, remains incompletely understood: changes in the envelope may impede dye penetration or reduce the cell’s ability to retain the dye after decolorization.

Here, knowing about the Koch paradox suggests a simple practical step. Imagine a familiar situation: the spleen or kidneys contain numerous granulomas, the appearance suggests mycobacteriosis, but no rods are detected with Ziehl–Neelsen staining. What next? We can stain the material again with fuchsin, but this time without acid decolorization.

Perhaps the irregular, unevenly stained rods with familiar morphology—previously undetectable after acid treatment—will become visible. We have not proved that they are mycobacteria, but we have obtained another observation and a new direction for investigation.

This is not an attempt to “fix a negative Ziehl–Neelsen result” at any cost. We are simply using another staining possibility: first examine the morphology without acid-based selection, then compare the finding with the tissue changes. Omitting acid broadens the range of structures that remain visible, so similar bacterial morphology alone does not establish a diagnosis.

Vilchèze and Kremer’s work concerns primarily M. tuberculosis. It is an important reason not to treat a negative ZN result as proof that mycobacteria are absent, but it is not a reason to automatically attribute every ZN-negative granuloma in a fish to dormant mycobacteria.

In other words, properly performed Ziehl–Neelsen staining does not answer the broad question “Are there any mycobacteria here?” It answers the narrower question: “Are there structures here that exhibit acid-fast staining under these conditions?”

The practical implication is important.

If rod-shaped bacteria retain staining after standard acid differentiation, we are observing acid-fastness—but we still have not identified their genus or species.
If no bacteria are detected with ZN staining, that does not yet prove that the specimen contains no mycobacteria.

During persistent infection, part of the bacterial population may remain invisible to standard acid-fast staining,
leading to an underestimation of the bacterial burden (Vilchèze & Kremer, 2017).

Staining does not tell us a bacterium’s name. It shows us how the bacterium behaves under a particular treatment.

This is where the importance of understanding a method’s limits becomes especially clear.

We see rods — we know that rod-shaped bacteria are present in the tissue.
We perform standard acid decolorization and the rods retain their staining — we observe acid-fastness, but still do not know their species.
We obtain a negative Ziehl–Neelsen result — we still do not know whether mycobacteria are absent from the tissue.

What do we actually know in the end?

Imagine a specimen in which numerous thin red rods become clearly visible after heating with fuchsin.

If their morphology allows us to confidently distinguish rod-shaped bacteria, we can describe their presence in the material examined. But not everything stained by fuchsin is necessarily a bacterium: other structures may be present in the preparation.

We can assess:

  • their shape and size;
  • their spatial arrangement;
  • their abundance and the density of clusters;
  • the tissue context;
  • their association with granulomatous or other pathological changes;
  • whether the finding is repeated in different areas of the specimen.

Morphology alone, of course, cannot establish the bacterial genus, let alone the species.

But a lack of complete identification does not make the observation useless.

Seeing bacteria and identifying their species are two different diagnostic tasks.

Similarly:

Seeing stained bacteria and demonstrating their acid-fastness are also two different tasks.

If a higher level of certainty is required, we can perform a complete acid-fast staining procedure, culture, PCR, or another confirmatory test.

But if those options are unavailable at the moment, that is no reason to pretend the microscope showed us nothing at all.


So where does “tradition instead of science” come in?

The standard method is not the problem.
A standard method is valuable precisely because its conditions are defined, tested, and reproducible.
The problem begins when we stop asking why each step is performed and merely repeat the familiar sequence like a ritual:

“You cannot see anything without phenol.”
“It has to be this particular acid because that is what the instructions say.”
“If one step is missing, the entire result is automatically worthless.”

A scientific approach asks a different question:

What exactly changed in the result when we changed the method?

If we do not understand what a stain actually demonstrates, we may never even think to examine the specimen in another way. If you fail to recognize the limits of your knowledge, you also fail to notice the questions you have yet to ask.

Remove acid decolorization, and you lose the right to claim acid-fastness, but you have not necessarily lost the diagnostic information.
You may even see structures that would have remained invisible after acid treatment.
Change the staining conditions, and you can assess whether you have obtained the image you needed.
Substitute another acid, and you can test which structures become decolorized and what the result allows you to claim.

Sometimes changing a method narrows the range of justified conclusions while expanding the range of things we can observe. The boundary is not between a “correct” and an “incorrect” ritual, but between what we have actually tested and what we merely assume.

You can change a method. But when you do, the limits of what you can legitimately conclude change with it.



How should the result be described?

If the classic procedure was not followed in full, we cannot hide behind its name.

Appropriate wording:
“A modified heated fuchsin stain without acid decolorization was used. Intensely stained rod-shaped bacteria were detected in the tissue material. Their acid-fastness was not assessed.”
Inappropriate wording:
“Ziehl–Neelsen staining was performed; acid-fast bacteria were detected.”

Those are two different claims.


Conclusion

Knowing a method’s limits means being able to control it.

If a method has been modified, there is no need either to pass it off as the standard procedure or to automatically dismiss the information it provides. Sometimes, by leaving out one step, we lose diagnostic selectivity but gain the opportunity to notice something we could not see before.

What we need to do is much simpler:

State precisely what we did, what we observed, and what actually follows from it.
