Twigs and Rods. Who Are We Looking at in This Specimen?


Depth of analysis
F. F.O. F.O.S.

For a long time, a specimen in my virtual microscope collection had a simple label: “Mycobacteria.” Red rod-shaped structures against a blue background, stained by the Ziehl–Neelsen method. Everything looked familiar—until I examined those structures more carefully.

Some were not merely elongated rods. They branched. And that raised a question: why had I decided they were mycobacteria in the first place?

Red rods are an observation. Mycobacteria are an identification. Between the two are several checks that are easy to overlook.

This article is not about identifying a pathogen from a single image. It is about why the familiar label “fish TB” can sometimes prevent us from noticing a much more interesting picture.


Are Actinomycetes Bacteria or Fungi?

Bacteria. The name “actinomycetes” is historically associated with filamentous, sometimes branching growth that resembles fungal mycelium. But the resemblance is superficial: neither Nocardia nor Mycobacterium is a fungus.

Both genera belong to the broad group Actinomycetota (actinomycetes) and the same order, Mycobacteriales, but to different families: Mycobacterium belongs to Mycobacteriaceae, whereas Nocardia belongs to Nocardiaceae (NCBI Taxonomy: Mycobacterium) (NCBI Taxonomy: Nocardia).

So “actinomycetes” is not another name for Nocardia, nor does it refer exclusively to filamentous bacteria. Mycobacteria are actinomycetes too, although they usually appear as individual rods.


What Do We Mean by “Twigs”?

Nocardia often grows as thin, branching filaments. These filaments can fragment, leaving separate short rods visible in a specimen. Moreover, cultured N. seriolae has been shown to change morphology with its growth phase: from filamentous cells to short rods and coccoid forms (Growth characteristics, 2010).

A rod-shaped appearance is more typical of mycobacteria. But shape does not provide an absolute boundary: some species and cell states can show atypical morphology, while overlapping bacteria can create the false impression of branching.

What is worth looking for under the microscope?

  • A continuous branch arising from a single filament rather than two separate rods crossing.
  • Filamentous structures with variable thickness and staining.
  • Fragmentation of filaments into shorter elements.
  • Repeated occurrence of similar morphology in different areas of the specimen.

These features help formulate a hypothesis, but none independently identifies the bacterial genus.

Why Are Some Bacteria Called “Partially Acid-Fast”?

It is easy to misunderstand this term.

Partial acid-fastness does not mean that a bacterium is only partly rod-shaped or must have patchy staining. It concerns how consistently a cell retains the primary dye after decolorization.

The cell envelopes of Mycobacterium and Nocardia contain mycolic acids, but their chemical composition and properties differ. As a result, many nocardiae exhibit weak or variable acid-fastness: the stain may be retained after mild decolorization but partly or completely lost after more stringent treatment. Modified procedures with gentler decolorization are therefore used to detect them (Brown-Elliott et al., 2006).

But there is no absolute rule that “standard Ziehl–Neelsen means mycobacteria, while mild acid means Nocardia.” Nocardia can retain the stain even after a Ziehl–Neelsen procedure. The result also depends on the species, physiological condition of the bacteria, and processing conditions.

In the publication on Nocardia seriolae in red drum, the authors presented Gram, Ziehl–Neelsen, and Fite–Faraco staining. They interpreted pale regions within the filamentous structures as evidence of partial acid-fastness (del Rio-Rodriguez et al., 2021). That is their interpretation of the images; pale interruptions in someone else’s smear are not, by themselves, a reliable way to distinguish bacterial genera.

If acid decolorization was omitted altogether, we cannot even call the structures “partially acid-fast.” We are looking at fuchsin-positive structures, but acid-fastness has not been tested.

For more on what each stage of staining actually demonstrates, see “Tradition Instead of Science I. The Method”.


A Case in Which “Mycobacteria” Turned Out to Be Nocardia

In their 2021 study, del Rio-Rodriguez and colleagues described a disease outbreak in farmed red drum (Sciaenops ocellatus) in Mexico. Affected fish showed ulcerative lesions and multiple pale nodules in their internal organs, particularly the kidneys and spleen. The farm recorded high cumulative mortality, reaching 70%.

In smears, scrapings, and tissue sections, the researchers observed long, branching, sometimes granular-staining bacteria that were Gram-positive and acid-fast. Such findings warranted a differential diagnosis between nocardiosis and mycobacteriosis. But the final identification as Nocardia seriolae did not come from the image: it relied on bacterial isolation, genus- and species-specific PCR, and gyrB gene sequencing (del Rio-Rodriguez et al., 2021).

Branching suggested looking for Nocardia. Identification required other methods.


What About Granulomas? Don’t They Mean “Fish TB”?

No. Granulomatous inflammation is a tissue response, not the name of a pathogen. Both mycobacteria and nocardiae can produce severe granulomatous lesions in fish. In their 2018 review, Maekawa and colleagues described nocardiosis as a disease affecting the skin, muscles, and internal organs of teleost fish (Maekawa et al., 2018).

Therefore, “pale nodules in the kidneys + red rods in a smear” does not automatically justify a diagnosis of “mycobacteriosis.” It justifies further investigation, not a confirmed species-level identification. Until we have a set of confirmed cases, we cannot claim that large rods in freshwater fish are more often nocardiae or that Nocardia and Mycobacterium occur in a “50/50” ratio. Observing different morphotypes across different specimens is a useful starting point for research, not a prevalence estimate.


How Do the Diseases Differ When We Consider Pathogenicity Rather Than Shape?

This is where the comparison becomes useful in practice. Accurate morphological description is not merely about giving a photograph a convincing caption. Similar-looking granulomatous lesions may be caused by different pathogens, with different epidemiology and antimicrobial susceptibility profiles.

Fish mycobacteriosis is most often described as a chronic, progressive infection. It may involve wasting, skin changes, lesions in the kidneys, spleen, and other organs, and the formation of granulomas. The causative species differ in growth rate and biological characteristics. For example, Mycobacterium marinum is a recognized fish pathogen that can also infect humans through damaged skin (Merck Veterinary Manual).

Nocardiosis can also be a chronic systemic infection. Common findings include multiple white nodules in organs, areas of necrosis, and skin lesions. Nocardia must not be considered less pathogenic merely because it is not Mycobacterium. Zhang and colleagues (2024), for example, investigated ten N. seriolae isolates recovered from freshwater fish—largemouth bass (Micropterus salmoides) and northern snakehead (Channa argus). In some experimentally infected groups, mortality reached 70–100% (Zhang et al., 2024).

This does not mean that every Nocardia infection will cause such mortality. Pathogenicity depends at least on the bacterial species and strain, the fish species and its condition, the route and dose of infection, and husbandry conditions. But the example demonstrates that the severity of disease alone cannot reliably distinguish nocardiosis from mycobacteriosis.

Pathogenicity should not be confused with the number of stained rods, either. More intense staining, longer filaments, or larger granulomas are not direct measures of virulence. Nor does mortality on a farm, by itself, identify the pathogen.

Resistance: To Acid, Antibiotics, or Environmental Conditions?

The word “resistance” may cause more confusion here than the micrographs themselves.

Acid-fastness is a staining characteristic. It describes retention of dye following a specified treatment and is influenced, among other factors, by the lipid-rich cell envelope. Antibiotic resistance is a different property: reduced susceptibility to a particular antimicrobial agent through specific cellular mechanisms. Persistence of infection within the host is yet another issue: a pathogen may remain in tissues and granulomatous lesions even when laboratory tests suggest susceptibility to certain drugs.

If a bacterium retains fuchsin after acid treatment, we have learned nothing about whether doxycycline, florfenicol, or another agent will work against it.

Both genera have cell envelopes containing mycolic acids, but this does not give them identical drug-susceptibility profiles. Relevant differences must be assessed at the species and isolate level. Likewise, merely detecting genes associated with possible resistance does not replace phenotypic susceptibility testing.

N. seriolae provides a useful example. In the Zhang et al. (2024) study, one of the most pathogenic isolates was resistant to the tested β-lactam agents but susceptible to several antibiotics of other classes (Zhang et al., 2024). In the study by Nguyen et al. (2026), 13 isolates from diseased fish showed reduced susceptibility to trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole according to the thresholds applied, whereas the florfenicol and oxytetracycline results fell within the wild-type population (Nguyen et al., 2026). These findings do not constitute a ready-to-use treatment table: the studies examined different isolates, used potentially different methods and interpretive criteria, and clinical breakpoints for many fish pathogens remain limited.

Fish mycobacteriosis is also difficult to manage. Beyond the pathogen’s biological properties, chronic infection and poorly accessible lesions complicate treatment. According to the Merck Veterinary Manual, treatments that reliably eradicate mycobacterial infection in fish are not available in routine practice (Merck Veterinary Manual).

Resistance to disinfectants is a third, separate question. A Ziehl–Neelsen result cannot tell us which disinfection procedure will work for the system, equipment, or surfaces. Such decisions require evidence concerning the active ingredient, concentration, contact time, and organic load.

What Does This Change in Aquarium Practice?

  • Diagnostic investigation. Branching Gram-positive structures with variable acid-fastness are a reason to include Nocardia in the differential diagnosis, rather than labeling every granuloma as mycobacteriosis.
  • Risk assessment. A confirmed pathogen, the extent of lesions, and the condition of other fish matter more than a single striking “twig” in a smear.
  • Specimen handling. When systemic bacterial infection is strongly suspected, appropriate material should ideally be retained for culture and molecular testing instead of relying solely on a red-stained slide.
  • Treatment. Even identification to genus level does not replace susceptibility testing or an assessment of whether an effective drug concentration can be reached in affected tissues without unjustified harm to the fish or the aquarium life-support system.
  • Staff safety. When handling fish suspected of having mycobacteriosis, the known zoonotic risk of certain species, especially M. marinum, must be considered (Merck Veterinary Manual). That level of risk should not automatically be attributed to every Nocardia species.

Now we can see why distinguishing “twigs” from “rods” matters. Not to diagnose by shape, but to select the right tests and avoid the wrong treatment decision.


What Should We Do with an Unusual Specimen?

Start by describing exactly what is visible rather than substituting the name of a bacterium.

Observation: fuchsin-positive rod-shaped or filamentous structures; note any apparent branching, granular staining, or isolated fragments.

Method: whether acid decolorization was performed, which procedure was used, and what controls were included. A positive Ziehl–Neelsen result demonstrates acid-fast staining under the conditions used, not bacterial identity.

Identification: if determining the pathogen is important to the diagnosis, additional evidence is needed—for example, Gram staining, bacterial isolation, or molecular identification. Even convincing branching on a micrograph does not replace confirmation.

An appropriate description of an unconfirmed finding might be:

“Fuchsin-positive rod-shaped and filamentous structures with apparent branching were observed in a spleen impression smear. The morphology is compatible with branching actinomycetes, including members of the genus Nocardia. Genus-level identification has not been established.”

If acid decolorization was not performed, that must be stated separately, and the structures must not be described as acid-fast.


Instead of a Conclusion

We are accustomed to seeing red rods and mentally adding “mycobacteria.”

Shape suggests what to investigate. Staining demonstrates a property under particular conditions. Naming the pathogen requires evidence.

That is precisely why we should show and document what is actually observed, even when definitive identification remains unavailable.
