Traditions Instead of Science IV. Diagnosis.


“Granulomas? Must be fish TB.”

When granulomas are found in the parenchymal organs of a fish, the next thought often comes almost automatically:

Mycobacteriosis.

Or, even more simply:

Fish tuberculosis.

The association is understandable. Granulomas are indeed very common in fish with mycobacteriosis. The problem begins when a characteristic host response is treated as the name of a pathogen. First, we need to establish what exactly we are looking at.


What is a granuloma?


One classical definition of a granuloma is a compact, organized collection of mature mononuclear phagocytes, primarily macrophages. Necrosis, giant cells, lymphocytes, and other familiar features may be present, but they are not essential. What matters is the organized accumulation of mature macrophages (Pagán & Ramakrishnan, 2018).

The modern definition can be simplified even further: A granuloma is an organized attempt by macrophages to deal with something a single macrophage could not handle.

Pagán and Ramakrishnan offer a particularly clear model: if the first phagocyte engulfs an object and successfully destroys it, there is no need for a granuloma at all.

If the object persists, resists degradation, or is too large, other cells are recruited and an organized structure begins to form (Pagán & Ramakrishnan, 2018). In other words, the presence of a granuloma tells us, first and foremost:

something here was persistent enough that an ordinary phagocytic response was not sufficient.

But it does not yet tell us what that something was.

It does not even have to be “foreign”

There is a particularly interesting detail here. As early as 1925, Margaret Reed Lewis observed the behavior of monocytes in culture. The cells responded not only to tuberculosis bacteria or experimentally introduced carbon particles.

They were also attracted to:

  • the body’s own melanin granules;
  • dead granulocytes;
  • erythrocytes.

After several days, macrophages that had engulfed material that was difficult to digest acquired an epithelioid appearance, and some developed into multinucleated giant cells (Pagán & Ramakrishnan, 2018). This leads to an important point. For a granulomatous response, the distinction is not simply:

“self versus non-self.”

A more useful question is:

can this material be removed quickly, or not?

A dead cell does not literally become a “foreign body.” It remains material belonging to the organism. But when a cell breaks down, membranes, lipids, proteins, and other cellular debris remain and must be cleared. If this material persists and continues to engage phagocytes, it too can become part of a chronic granulomatous response.

That is why the formula:

“granuloma = infectious agent”

is too simplistic from the outset.

A mechanism older than fish

The granulomatous response turns out to be far older than one might expect from the complex cellular immune system of vertebrates. Granulomas are formed not only by mammals, birds, and fish. They have also been described in mollusks, worms, and insects (Pagán & Ramakrishnan, 2018). One example is especially striking. A parasitoid wasp lays an egg inside a Drosophila larva. The egg is too large for a single phagocyte to engulf, so cells surround it and form an organized granulomatous structure. The task remains the same:

if you cannot destroy it, isolate it.

In the freshwater clam Corbicula fluminea, a granulomatous response can even develop around its own larvae when, for some reason, they are not released normally. Their remains are gradually resorbed (Pagán & Ramakrishnan, 2018). It is hard to imagine a clearer example of why a granuloma, by itself, tells us nothing about the presence of mycobacteria.

Many possible causes

Very different agents can provoke a granulomatous response. In their review, Pagán and Ramakrishnan list bacteria, fungi, protozoa, helminths, and viruses among infectious causes. Granulomas can also form around foreign material and in a range of noninfectious inflammatory conditions (Pagán & Ramakrishnan, 2018).

So the reasoning:

granuloma → mycobacterium

does not work.

A better sequence is:

granuloma → persistent factor → determine what that factor is.

In aquarium practice, mycobacteriosis is indeed one of the first possibilities to investigate when multiple granulomas are found. But the leading differential diagnosis is not yet an established diagnosis.


And this is where mycobacteria enter the picture

The story becomes even more interesting with mycobacteria. For a long time, granulomas were imagined almost as fortresses built by the host around bacteria:

here are the bacteria, surrounded by a wall of immune cells — the infection is contained.

This mechanism does exist. But it turned out to be only half the picture. Studies of Mycobacterium marinum in zebrafish showed that pathogenic mycobacteria can exploit the very process of granuloma formation (Pagán & Ramakrishnan, 2015). An infected macrophage recruits more macrophages. It dies. Another macrophage engulfs its remains, together with the bacteria inside. Now the next macrophage is infected as well, and it too becomes part of the granuloma. In experiments, the number of infected macrophages began to rise sharply after cellular aggregates formed. At the same time, the number of viable mycobacteria increased (Pagán & Ramakrishnan, 2015).

The result is almost a paradox:

the host gathers cells to contain the infection, while the bacteria use those incoming cells as new sites for replication.

Moreover, a mature granuloma is not necessarily a sealed capsule. Cells continue to enter and leave it, and infected macrophages can depart from the primary focus and contribute to the formation of new ones (Pagán & Ramakrishnan, 2015).

This makes the modern understanding of a granuloma much more interesting than the old view:

a granuloma can both contain an infection and help the pathogen spread.

Which effect predominates depends on the stage of the process, the host immune response, and the properties of the pathogen.

Many granulomas — many bacteria?

Not necessarily.

And the reverse assumption is no more reliable:

Few granulomas — few bacteria.

A granuloma reflects more than just the amount of pathogen present. It reflects the nature of the interaction between the host and the pathogen. When the cellular response is effectively organized, well-defined granulomas may develop. When macrophage function is impaired, lesions can become disorganized and necrotic even while the bacterial burden is very high. In experimental models, disruption of TNF-dependent mechanisms was accompanied by massive mycobacterial proliferation and loss of normal granuloma architecture (Pagán & Ramakrishnan, 2015).

Thus, the situation:

generalized mycobacteriosis, but hardly any of those neat, numerous granulomas

is not inconsistent with the diagnosis at all. Sometimes it may indicate the opposite: the system intended to contain the infection is functioning poorly. Conversely, numerous granulomas do not prove the presence of mycobacteria.

What if we use Ziehl–Neelsen staining?

This takes us from suspicion to the next level of evidence.

If numerous rod-shaped bacteria are found inside granulomas and retain carbol fuchsin after acid decolorization, the case for mycobacteriosis becomes considerably stronger. But it is still important not to skip another step. Ziehl–Neelsen staining demonstrates acid-fastness. It does not automatically identify the species of bacterium.

In other words:

granuloma

does not equal:

mycobacterium,

and:

acid-fast rod-shaped bacterium

still does not automatically equal:

the genus Mycobacterium.

Species-level identification requires other methods: culture, PCR, sequencing, or a combination of these. This is precisely why diagnostic methods should not be treated as rituals. Each subsequent method answers a different question. A granuloma tells us:

an organized chronic tissue response is taking place.

A stain tells us:

structures with particular staining properties are present here.

Molecular or culture-based methods bring us closer to answering:

what exactly is present here?

One does not replace the other.

Sometimes a granuloma has nothing to do with bacteria

This is especially useful to remember in routine microscopy. One can encounter numerous granulomatous lesions without finding any acid-fast bacteria in them. Entirely different processes may be responsible, including parasitic infections. That is why it is better to describe what is actually visible first:

“multiple granulomas”

and only then investigate their etiology.

Because the entry:

“multiple granulomas — mycobacteriosis”

quietly turns an observation into a diagnosis.

A granuloma is more than just a “nodule” in a tissue. Its organization also matters. The morphology of a lesion often depends on what is at its center and how many separate centers are present. Several patterns can be distinguished for illustration.


  1. A single compact center If the initiating factor is small, the response may organize around one center and produce a relatively round, compact granuloma.
  1. A large or irregularly shaped center If the underlying object is large, uneven, or difficult to remove — for example, a parasitic structure, a concretion, or a substantial area of tissue breakdown — the granuloma may follow its contours and become less regular in shape.
  1. Two adjacent centers Sometimes two lesions lie close together. Their outer reactive zones may merge, giving the appearance of one complex granuloma. But two distinct centers indicate two neighboring granulomas rather than a single irregular one.

Another important feature is whether necrosis is present in the center.

If the central part of a lesion retains its cellular architecture instead of turning into amorphous debris, the granuloma may tentatively be considered non-necrotizing. If degenerating material accumulates centrally, cell outlines are lost, and an unstructured mass develops, it is a necrotizing granuloma. Necrosis itself also has diagnostic significance. Central necrosis is quite characteristic of mature mycobacterial granulomas and strengthens suspicion of mycobacteriosis. However, it is not pathognomonic: necrotizing granulomas can also arise from other infections. Conversely, the absence of necrosis does not rule out mycobacteriosis.

Some fungal and parasitic infections may produce numerous well-organized granulomas without central necrosis, even in severe generalized disease.

Within a single epizootic process, granulomas often have similar architecture. When the same agent affects a relatively homogeneous group of fish, the pattern of the granulomatous response — its shape, capsule development, and presence or absence of necrosis — may recur quite consistently. However, that morphotype reflects the interaction between a particular pathogen and a particular host; it is not a universal property of the pathogen.


The scale of the “wrapping”

It is important to examine not only the center of a granuloma, but also how it is separated from the surrounding tissue. In some cases, the peripheral zone is barely developed. In others, a conspicuous multilayered structure forms around the lesion, consisting of macrophages, other inflammatory cells, and connective-tissue elements. The longer a lesion persists, and the more resistant the factor at its center, the more organized this “wrapping” may become. But there is no universal rule: the degree of capsule development depends on the nature of the irritant, the duration of the process, and the condition of the host tissue.


So where are the “traditions instead of science”?

Not in the association between granulomas and mycobacteriosis itself. That association is real and extremely useful in practice. The problem begins when a likely cause becomes the only possible explanation simply because we are accustomed to seeing the two together.

The shortcut:

saw a granuloma → wrote “tuberculosis”

saves time.

But it discards the most interesting part of diagnosis. What caused the granuloma to form? Are there bacteria inside it? Are they acid-fast? Is there an infectious agent at all? What is happening in the surrounding tissue? Is this a localized or generalized process? Only after asking these questions can we begin to move from a morphological finding toward a diagnosis.

A granuloma is not a diagnosis.
It is a trace of the work done by the host.
The task of diagnosis is to find out why that work was needed.
