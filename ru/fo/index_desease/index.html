FMC: Why a Treatment Bath Is Not the Same as Effective Treatment


Глубина разбора
F. F.O. F.O.S.

FMC

In aquarium fish care, FMC sometimes becomes the answer before the question has even been formulated. A fish rubs against decorations, mucus production increases, a parasitic infection is suspected—and a treatment bath is prescribed.

This does not necessarily mean the decision is wrong. FMC does contain biologically active substances. The problem arises when the name of the mixture replaces the rationale for using it, and performing a bath is taken as proof that treatment was effective.

To distinguish between these situations, we need to understand what FMC contains, where its treatment regimens came from, and which parameters determine the outcome.


What Is FMC?

In the formulation discussed here, FMC combines three components:

  • Formalin — an aqueous solution of formaldehyde, used against certain external pathogens of fish.
  • Malachite green — a dye with antiparasitic activity.
  • Methylene blue — another dye with its own biological activity.

The mixture may act on several biological targets. However, its components have toxic effects as well as useful biological activity. The argument that “three substances are present, so at least one is bound to work” does not stand up to scrutiny.

Even when the composition is known, the name FMC alone cannot tell us which regimen will be both safe and effective. We also need to know the concentration, duration of exposure, condition of the fish, characteristics of the suspected pathogen, and water conditions.


Where Did the Treatment Regimen Come From?

The full F.O.S. article examines the history of the mixture through its sources. An early precursor was a combination of malachite green and formalin described by Leteux and Meyer in 1972 for controlling Ichthyophthirius and other protozoan parasites of fish ( publication).

According to Gerald Bassleer (2010), he proposed a three-component FMC formulation in 1979. The stock solution described contained 3.5 g each of malachite green and methylene blue per litre of formalin. The regimen given was 1 mL of stock solution per 100 L of water, with exposure lasting approximately two days; for ichthyophthiriasis, another treatment was to follow a water change.

This is a historical account of a specific formulation and regimen, not a universal instruction for treating any fish. Its relevance here is different: it involved prolonged contact with a relatively dilute mixture, not an arbitrarily chosen ten-minute bath.

For comparison, the same reference separately described two regimens for formalin alone: 1–2 mL per 100 L for 12–48 hours, and 0.125–0.25 mL per litre for 30–60 minutes.

We must not conflate formulations when comparing them: formalin regimens cannot automatically be treated as FMC regimens. Even with a single active substance, changing both the concentration and the duration produces a different exposure regimen.


Why Exposure Time Cannot Be Separated from Concentration

Exposure time is the period during which the fish remains in contact with the preparation. Concentration tells us how much of the active substance is present in the water. Together, they define the exposure conditions, but they do not in themselves guarantee effectiveness.

A statement such as “ten minutes in FMC” tells us too little to assess the outcome unless we know:

  1. the exact formulation and actual concentration of the solution;
  2. the duration of contact and water conditions;
  3. which organism or pathological process the treatment is intended to eliminate;
  4. how susceptible that target is to the chosen exposure;
  5. whether the individual fish can tolerate the procedure;
  6. what was observed before and after treatment.

The parasite’s life-cycle stage also matters. If the susceptible stage is inaccessible to the substance during a short bath, the presence of an active preparation does not mean the target has been reached. Whether the parasite is on the body surface or within tissues also affects its likelihood of coming into contact with the substance.

Therefore, this argument does not work: “Formalin acts against ectoparasites; FMC contains formalin; therefore, ten minutes in FMC is enough.” The essential link is missing: evidence for that particular regimen against the suspected target.

A preparation is defined not only by its composition but also by the conditions under which it is used. Treatment is justified when the regimen matches the specific objective.


What Does Improvement After a Bath Mean?

Imagine a fish that rubs intensely against objects and produces large amounts of mucus before treatment. The day after a bath, the irritation appears reduced and the fish looks better.

That is an observation. But several explanations are possible:

  • some external parasites may have died or become detached;
  • the amount of mucus on the body surface may have decreased;
  • water conditions may have changed when the fish was transferred;
  • contact with a possible source of infection may have been reduced;
  • the fish’s behaviour may have changed for reasons unrelated to the preparation.

Without an initial diagnostic assessment and a follow-up examination, we cannot determine which explanation applies. Even if the parasite burden has genuinely decreased, this does not prove that the pathogen has been completely eliminated or that the effect will persist.

A common reasoning error occurs here: “the fish improved after treatment” becomes “the treatment eliminated the cause of the disease.” Evidence is needed to bridge these two statements.


What Should Be Checked Before and After?

Before evaluating a treatment bath, it is useful to formulate a working hypothesis—for example, that the fish has an external parasitic infestation. Where feasible, microscopic examination of suitable material can be performed on the living fish, documenting the suspected pathogen, the severity of the lesions, and the fish’s clinical condition.

The next step is to define not merely a preparation but an objective: to reduce or eliminate a particular parasite burden at an acceptable level of risk to the animal. After treatment, we check whether that objective was achieved, rather than looking only for an improvement in appearance.

If there was no initial examination, conclusions about efficacy will be limited. If the suspected pathogen was not assessed before and after treatment, we cannot confidently say it has disappeared.

The same applies when a fish rubs against objects. Rubbing is not, by itself, a specific sign of parasitic disease and does not automatically determine either the diagnosis or the treatment regimen.


Why a Multicomponent Mixture Requires Extra Care

Several active substances can tempt us into thinking: “If one does not work, another will.”

But the other side of that reasoning is additional limitations and a potential toxic burden. If the chosen exposure time is too short to affect the parasite but long enough to irritate the fish’s skin or gills, the procedure may worsen the fish’s condition without solving the original problem.

This is not an argument against FMC itself. The mixture may be justified when the target, suitable regimen, and criteria for evaluating the outcome are known. The problem is prescribing it without those conditions.


Where Does Treatment End and Ritual Begin?

A reasoned approach to treatment follows a sequence:

Observation → working hypothesis → biological target → choice of preparation and regimen → evaluation of the outcome.

If we start at step four and then treat any improvement as confirmation of the diagnosis, we end up in a circular argument. The fish was rubbing against objects → FMC was administered → the fish rubs less → therefore, “FMC treats rubbing.” Yet the original cause remains unknown.

A treatment bath is a procedure that has been performed. Treatment efficacy is a conclusion that requires evidence.

The age of the preparation is beside the point. An old remedy does not become wrong simply because it is old, but neither does long-standing practice prove that an arbitrarily chosen regimen is effective.
